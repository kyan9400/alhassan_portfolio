3:T64f,Elektroservis keeps its engineering (PTO) documentation in a network archive of about 4.1k PDF, DOCX, XLSX and RTF files, about 1.5k of them with text. From April to October 2026, under civil-law contracts, I built a search service over it: an engineer types a cable mark, a document code, an equipment designation or a phrase and gets the document, the PDF page (or Excel sheet and rows, Word section), a text fragment and the network path. Documents never leave the company network.

Search is hybrid, with no LLM generation: a custom inverted index over words and designations, plus FAISS semantic search on sentence-transformers embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2). Designations are matched by general rules: look-alike Latin and Cyrillic letters, spaces and hyphens do not break a match, while numbers and marks are never substituted. Scans are detected automatically and selected pages go through Tesseract + Poppler OCR. The backend is FastAPI, the interface is plain HTML/JS, and it runs as a systemd service on Ubuntu Server with an atomic index swap and rollback.

Code and mark queries went from 9–10 s to 1.2–1.7 s and phrase queries from 55–70 s to 2–4 s; after the move to the inverted index, p95 is 0.18 s on 55 control queries. Quality is held by 739 pytest tests and a 104-query control set with top-1/top-5 metrics. I built it with AI agents (Codex, Cursor, Claude Code): I gathered the requirements, wrote the agent briefs, reviewed the changes and checked them on the production index. The client accepted the work in September 2026. Over 11 chaos scenarios and 40,461 requests (4,188 of them sent twice at once), a checker found nothing lost and nothing done twice. Load tests found the hot spots: the ledger does about 430 transfers a second on one CPU, shedding the excess instead of falling over.

A LightGBM model on real-time features from Kafka caught 88% of fraud at 0.7% false declines against 37% for tuned rules, measured against a simulator's hidden truth; ClickHouse analytics agree with the ledger to the kopeck. Merchants get a gRPC API, signed webhooks and a TypeScript SDK: with the service killed mid-payment, all 1,085 changes still reached the example shop.
