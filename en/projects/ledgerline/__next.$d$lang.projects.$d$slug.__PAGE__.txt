3:T5ae,Ledgerline is an open-source payments platform I built with Claude Code to show what a bank's core needs: money that is never created, lost or moved twice, even when everything around it fails. A double-entry ledger in Kotlin and Spring Boot keeps an append-only event log in PostgreSQL; balances are projections rebuilt from it, every write is idempotent, and an audit recomputes the books. Card payments, refunds and payouts run as state machines and sagas on top of it, and every change reaches Kafka through a transactional outbox.

It is proven by breaking it while a client keeps paying: services, PostgreSQL and Kafka killed, networks cut, answers cut off mid-way. Over 11 chaos scenarios and 40,461 requests (4,188 of them sent twice at once), a checker found nothing lost and nothing done twice. Load tests found the hot spots: the ledger does about 430 transfers a second on one CPU, shedding the excess instead of falling over.

A LightGBM model on real-time features from Kafka caught 88% of fraud at 0.7% false declines against 37% for tuned rules, measured against a simulator's hidden truth; ClickHouse analytics agree with the ledger to the kopeck. Merchants get a gRPC API, signed webhooks and a TypeScript SDK: with the service killed mid-payment, all 1,085 changes still reached the example shop. Traces follow a payment through the outbox, the worker and webhooks, and every alert was proven to fire by injecting the fault it watches.4:T5a3,Proofbench is an open-source evaluation platform I built with Claude Code for an LLM agent that can do damage: a customer-support agent for a small shop that refunds money, cancels orders and reads other people's data. Almost every question worth asking about it has an exact answer, so it is decided from an effect log rather than by asking a model: what moved, by how much, on which order, whether something forbidden was attempted even if the shop refused it, and whether another customer appeared in the reply.

The agent is qwen3.5:9b running locally on one GPU. On the baseline it was fully correct on 75.0% of attempts and did nothing dangerous on 95.8%. The dominant failure is an agent that reasons correctly and then never calls the tool. Run with more randomness, the pass rate stayed exactly the same while the safety rate fell to 83.3%: it paid out on two of five planted instructions, which is why the two rates are kept apart.

Reply quality is the one thing a model judges, and the judge is measured: against five blind model raters it agreed at kappa 0.93 (95% CI 0.78–1.0). Recorded runs replay through the graders in 4 s instead of 18 minutes of GPU, and a CI gate fails if any grader's counts move, after a test showed the first version passing with a critical grader switched off. A Go simulator drives 240 vans, trucks and minibuses on the Moscow road network from OpenStreetMap, reports like GPS trackers do, and injects faults: fuel theft, failing thermostats and batteries, GPS spoofing, night use. What really happened is published apart, so detectors can be scored without seeing it.

Readings flow through NATS JetStream into TimescaleDB (13.4 million replayed with no gaps or repeats across restarts) and out to a 3D twin built with React, MapLibre and three.js. A FastAPI alerter finds faults as they happen, and LightGBM models on daily features warn of breakdowns: on unseen fleets they caught 156 of 157 overheating failures 4.5 days ahead, against 3.0 days for threshold rules, with fewer false alarms. A copilot on a local Qwen 3.5 9B can only call seven validated, read-only queries and shows the data behind each answer; it answered 17 of 18 held-out questions correctly.

Drivers use an Expo app that queues shifts, inspections and defect reports with photos offline and syncs them in order without duplicates; defects reach the twin at once.
